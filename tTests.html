t Tests

A lot of statistics is about figuring out where the average value (called \(\mu\)) of a whole group lies, based on a certain type of math model. One popular way to do this is by using a test number (called a test statistic) that follows something known as a t distribution.

One Sample t Test

A one-sample t test is used when you have a guess (called a hypothesis) about the average value (\(\mu\)) of just one number-based variable.

Overview

Questions

The one sample t test can be used to answer questions like:

  • How long does it take to drive from Rexburg, ID to Salt Lake City, UT on average?
  • Is human body temperature really 98.6&deg; F on average?
  • Do I spend less than $3 a day, on average, purchasing snacks?

Requirements

This test only works well if both of the following are true:

  1. The sample represents the whole group you’re studying. The best way to make sure of this is to randomly choose people or things for the sample.

  2. The average of the sample, written as \(\bar{x}\), can be assumed to follow a normal shape. This is usually okay if either:

  1. The data from the whole group looks like a normal distribution when you check it using a Q-Q Plot, or

  2. The sample size is pretty big (at least \(n > 30\)), though what counts as “big” depends on how uneven or skewed the data is.

If these two things are true, then you can trust the test to give useful info about the whole group. If not, that doesn’t automatically mean the test results are wrong—but you can’t be sure they’re right either.

Hypotheses

Math Code 
$$
  H_0: \mu = 5.2
$$

$$
  H_a: \mu \neq 5.2
$$

\(H_0: \mu = \text{some number}\)

\(H_a: \mu \ \left\{\underset{<}{\stackrel{>}{\neq}}\right\} \ \text{some number}\)

Examples: analysis resubmits

Explanation

A one-sample t test is often used when you want to test a guess about the average (\(\mu\)) of a single group. It’s the right choice when the average of the sample is expected to follow a normal shape, and the sample is a simple random pick from the whole group.

In the picture below, the null hypothesis (\(H_0: \mu = \mu_0\)) is shown as a normal (bell-shaped) curve in gray, centered at \(\mu_0\). Here, \(\mu_0\) is just a number that we’re guessing might be the true average. This picture shows that the null hypothesis is basically saying, “we think the middle (average) of the data is at \(\mu_0\).”

Once you’ve made a null hypothesis and also stated the alternative hypothesis, you take a simple random sample of size \(n\) from the group you’re studying. In the picture above, the blue dots show this sample, and they are centered around their average, written as \(\bar{x}\).

Above those dots is a second bell-shaped curve (the blue dashed line) that represents what the data might look like if the alternative hypothesis were true. It shows the idea that maybe this new curve fits the data better than the one we assumed with the null hypothesis.

The job of the one-sample t test is to figure out how likely it is to get a sample average as far away from the guessed value (\(\mu_0\)) as the one we actually got—or even more extreme—if the null hypothesis is true. That likelihood is called the p-value.

This all works because we’re assuming the average of the sample follows a normal distribution. That’s why the test uses something called the t distribution. In this case, the distribution of the test statistic t, \[ t = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{s/\sqrt{n}} \]
is known to follow a t distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom. (The math behind this is amazing! You can check out an advanced stats textbook if you’re curious about how it works.)

The p-value in a one-sample t test is the chance of getting a t value as extreme—or more extreme—than the one from your sample, assuming it follows a t distribution with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom.

If this p-value is really small (smaller than a cutoff number called \(\alpha\)), then we say the alternative hypothesis seems more believable, and we reject the null hypothesis in its favor.

Paired Samples t Test

The paired samples t test is used when you have two related sets of data, and you’re testing a guess about the average difference between them, written as \(\mu_d\).

Overview

Questions

The Paired Samples t Test can be used to answer questions like:

  • From pre-test to post-test is there an improvement on average in the subjects?
  • How much taller are husbands than their wives, on average?
  • Do hospital patients that are carefully matched together according to reason for being in the hospital, age, gender, ethnicity, height, and weight show increased stay times in the hospital when infected with a nosocomial infection compared to those who were not infected?

Requirements

This test only works well if both of the following are true:

  1. The sample of differences (the differences between the paired values) should represent the whole population of differences.

  2. The average of those differences, written as \(\bar{d}\) (which is like \(\bar{x}\) but for the differences), should be normally distributed. This is usually okay if:

  1. A Q-Q Plot shows the differences look like a normal distribution, or

  2. The number of differences (\(n\)) is large enough.

Hypotheses

Math Code 
$$
  H_0: \mu_d = 0
$$

$$
  H_a: \mu_d \neq 0
$$

\(H_0: \mu_d = \text{some number, but typically 0}\)
\(H_a: \mu_d \ \left\{\underset{<}{\stackrel{>}{\neq}}\right\} \ \text{some number, but typically 0}\)

Examples: sleepPaired studentPaired

Explanation

The paired samples t test looks at the average of all the differences between the paired values. So, it basically becomes a one-sample t test, but just on those differences. That’s why we still need to assume that the average of the differences, written as \(\bar{d}\), follows a normal distribution. (Also, the differences should come from a simple random sample of all the possible differences.)

This test is similar to the independent samples t test, but with a key difference: here, we have extra info that lets us pair each value in one group with a matching value in the other group. This pairing helps us clearly see the change or difference each individual went through between the two sets.

In the picture below, the dots show how values are matched up (paired), and what really matters are the differences between those pairs.

Independent Samples t Test

The independent samples t test is used when you want to test a guess about the difference between the averages of two different groups, written as \(\mu_1 - \mu_2\).

Overview

Questions

The Independent Samples t Test can be used to answer questions like:

  • Are boys taller than girls on average?
  • Do students who show up to class everyday get higher scores on average than those who don’t?
  • Do you take more steps on average on weekdays or on weekends?

Requirements

This test only works well if both of the following are true:

  1. Both samples should represent the whole population. The best way to make sure of this is to use simple random samples.

  2. The difference between the sample averages, written as \((\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)\), should be normally distributed. This is usually safe to assume if:

  • Each group has at least 30 data points, or

  • The data from each group looks normal when checked with a Q-Q Plot.

Hypotheses

Math Code 
$$
  H_0: \mu_\text{label 1} - \mu_\text{label 2} = 13.2
$$

$$
  H_a: \mu_\text{label 1} - \mu_\text{label 2} \neq 13.2
$$

\(H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = \text{some number, but typically 0}\)

\(H_a: \mu_1 - \mu_2 \ \left\{\underset{<}{\stackrel{>}{\neq}}\right\} \ \text{some number, but typically 0}\)

Examples: sleepInd student1 student2

Explanation

The first picture below shows a case where the two normal curves (distributions) have different averages, called \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\). That means the real difference between the two groups is not zero.

It’s important to know that we don’t actually know the exact values of \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\). All we have are two separate samples of data (the blue dots) with sizes \(n_1\) and \(n_2\).

In this example, the null hypothesis—\(H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0\) (which means we think both group averages are equal)—gets rejected. Instead, we go with the alternative hypothesis: \(H_a: \mu_1 - \mu_2 \neq 0\), which says the averages are not equal.

That’s the right choice in this case, because the true difference between the two group averages (\(\mu_1 - \mu_2\)) really isn’t zero.

When the null hypothesis is true, meaning \(H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0\) (the group averages are equal), then the test statistic \(t\)—which is found by comparing the two sample means (\(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2\)) and adjusting for sample size—follows a t distribution.

The number of degrees of freedom is less than or equal to \(n_1 + n_2 - 2\).

The p-value in the independent samples t test comes from this t distribution. It tells us the chance of getting a test statistic as extreme—or even more extreme—than the one we got, assuming the null hypothesis is true. \[ t = \frac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}{\sqrt{s_1/n_1+s_2/n_2 }} \]
